# Tales of the Dark Moon — Season Audit Report

**Scope:** Weeks 1–6, including the final Gathering and the post-finale explanation of Garrick’s secret.  
**Reviewer stance:** Independent senior game designer and story editor. This is a hard audit, not a reassurance document.  
**Status:** Editorial review of the supplied scripts. Numerical balance and implementation still require separate simulation and playtesting.

---

## Executive verdict

**Season rating: 7.3/10 — strong enough to keep building, not ready to ship unchanged.**

The season has a real story engine. Its clues accumulate, its locations have identity, its emotional motifs recur with purpose, and the final twist changes the meaning of earlier scenes instead of merely adding a new villain. Garrick is the season’s strongest design asset: he can be warm without being innocent, honest without being candid, and present throughout the journey without announcing his importance. The extra cup, the repeated family sayings, his knowledge of place names, and his insistence that “the Stones” wake the king all gain new meanings after the finale.

The central problem is structural: **the narrative is written as if players follow one intended route, while the mechanics allow many different routes.** Optional successes, Challenge failures, combat defeats and player choices can skip details which later recaps, maps or Gatherings assume the player has received. That inconsistency recurs in every part of the season. It is not an isolated proofreading issue; it is a content/state problem that can break the campaign’s critical path.

The second major issue is the recurring Gathering modifier that says the party’s Approach is harder if it solved the week’s map. The map is described as help and is one of the main rewards for weekday play, yet solving it increases the DC. This appears in all six Gatherings. Unless there is an intentional, clearly explained reason for making success a penalty, this is a systematic rules bug.

The third issue is delivery load. Individual weekday chapters are short and readable, but the cumulative Campfire loop asks for several interpretive pins, a recurring theory question, riddle attempts, a “case so far” response, an open question, and daily recap material. The final Gathering then stacks combat, United Effort, lore, matching, major choices and a cliffhanger into an advertised 20–30 minutes. The writing deserves breathing room; the current amount of interaction may leave too little.

**The right decision is not to rewrite the story. It is to protect the story by repairing route logic, state tracking, rules clarity and session pacing.**

## The season’s strongest achievement

### Garrick’s secret works in principle

The post-finale note establishes that Garrick is the last living descendant of Malakor and the hidden leader of the Order. His plan depends on the party: their Seeker blood finds and wakes the four Stones; the maps reveal where those Stones lie; and the rite which sealed the king is also the rite that wakes him. He has used the truth, narrowed answers and withheld meanings rather than straightforward lies.

That design has several strong effects:

- **The re-read changes earlier scenes.** “My grandmother taught me that name,” “My family’s been in these hills longer than the roads have,” and “It’s the Stones that wake him” are facts which sounded like local knowledge or helpful expertise. After the finale, they expose his lineage and motive.
- **His warmth does not have to be fake.** The note says he genuinely meant it when he told the players they saved the children. Their rescues are real victories, even though those victories also help his ritual. This is much more interesting than revealing that every kind act was an illusion.
- **The extra cup is a proper long-running motif.** It first reads as Garrick’s habit and hospitality. It eventually becomes an offering to the absent king and a sign that Garrick’s family has kept watch for generations.
- **The title “moon-blood” is a fair reinterpretation, if the clues are supported in play.** It was the king’s title and then his family’s—not a generic label for children born during the dark moon. The players have made a plausible inference, but the story never has to rely on Garrick telling a direct falsehood.
- **The final event turns the original mission back on the players.** They save every child, but their blood and maps help wake Malakor. The cliffhanger lands because the rescue story remains true while its wider meaning changes.

This is worth protecting. It is the reason to fix the mechanics rather than reduce the story to a simpler, more literal plot.

### What must be true for the twist to feel fair

A twist can be surprising without being random. It cannot rely entirely on an author’s private explanation shown only after the reveal. The after-finale recap is useful, but it does not by itself prove that every implication was perceptible to players beforehand.

The story should pass three tests:

1. **Reread test:** After Garrick’s identity is revealed, the original lines still mean what the recap says they mean. Most do.
2. **Evidence test:** Before the reveal, attentive players can point to several specific clues that make suspicion reasonable. There are good seeds—his grandmother’s knowledge of Malakor’s name, his family’s old ties, the Order’s sayings, his precise knowledge of Meira’s work, and the survival pattern—but some conclusions may currently rely too heavily on the recap’s explanations.
3. **Agency test:** Players can suspect Garrick, question him and interpret evidence without the script quietly ignoring their route. This is where the current system is weakest. If an optional clue was never shown, the player cannot fairly be expected to use it as evidence later.

### Hindsight findings: what the post-finale note changes

**1. The first ambush is no longer just a threat; it is a sign that Garrick’s people are managing the party’s danger.** The Week 4 line—“Every time they’ve had knives out at you, not one of you has died”—becomes more than reassurance. The Order needs the players alive and free to find the Stones. The game should make that distinction legible without making every fight toothless.

**2. Meira’s fate needs a careful fairness check.** The recap says Garrick listened to Meira for two years and knew when she had found enough to be worth killing. His Week 1 answers avoid saying he had no part in her disappearance or attack. That is a strong retrospective interpretation. But if the script wants players to regard the murder as fairly planted—not merely explained afterward—there should be a clue before the finale tying the attack to the Order’s need to control the information. The recap alone cannot substitute for evidence in the season itself.

**3. The party’s apparent success is also the antagonist’s progress.** Solving each map lets the Order locate a Stone. That is a powerful reversal, but it needs at least one sturdy on-screen causal clue that explains how the Order knows what the party found. “Closer to you than your own shadows” is a promising seed; it must be clear enough that the post-finale explanation feels like the answer to a question, not new rules introduced at the end.

**4. “The children were never in danger of dying” changes how earlier peril reads.** The author’s note says the children were required alive until the blooding. That creates a moral complication: Garrick can mean his kindness and still lead the party into an operation that exploits the children. It also means the scripts should not accidentally promise lethal jeopardy and then rely on an undisclosed guarantee. Preserve actual danger and consequence, but make the Order’s constraints consistent.

**5. “The faithful don’t [lie]” is an excellent final echo, but precision must not become loophole writing.** Garrick’s statements are mostly carefully bounded, and that is the point. If every line survives only because a lawyer can parse it narrowly, players will feel tricked instead of impressed. Keep the most telling omissions; cut or simplify the ones whose only job is to defend the twist after the fact.

## Whole-season patterns

### 1. The critical path is not protected

This is the number-one release risk. The rule “both branches reach the same plot” is a good principle, but it is not enough if the plot assumes that players found a specific clue, fragment or map. Several scripts appear to treat a challenge outcome or optional Press On as a gate to a required story fact. Later recaps silently promote that fact into a shared memory.

**Required design rule:** Every required fact, fragment and next location must be reachable after every legal outcome. Success can give an advantage, cost less time, grant more detail or improve the party’s position. Failure can cost gold, time, a resource or safety. Neither should erase a necessary piece of the story.

This does **not** mean every route must be identical. It means every route must be complete.

### 2. The game does not consistently distinguish “extra detail” from “essential clue”

Press On is described as a harder optional action that yields one more detail. In practice, some Press On branches contain fragments, route discoveries or facts needed by a later chapter. This turns an optional bonus into a hidden mandatory test.

**Fix:** Label every piece of information internally:

- **Critical:** needed to understand or enter the next story beat; guaranteed.
- **Useful:** gives context, changes the next scene, or offers a benefit; accessible through success but has an alternate path if missed.
- **Colour:** atmosphere, character or world texture; it may be missed safely.

Then validate every day’s branches against the next day’s opening recap and all later mechanics.

### 3. Recaps are acting as unreliable state machines

The daily “road so far” text is valuable for players who miss a day, but it sometimes asserts optional events as if they happened to everyone. That is particularly damaging in an asynchronous chat game: players may not remember a chapter, may have missed a branch, or may join late.

**Fix:** Recaps should use persistent state, not authorial intent. If the player found the fragment, say so. If only some friends saw the clue, share it as a reported observation (“Mei saw…”). If the game intentionally makes a clue common knowledge regardless of route, award it as common knowledge in the actual gameplay state.

### 4. The map reward is contradicted by the Gathering rule

Every Gathering repeatedly uses the modifier “+2 if party solved the week’s map.” The rules brief says the map helps at the Gathering. A higher DC makes success harder. The fact this survives all six weeks suggests a copied template, not six individual creative choices.

**Fix:** Correct the source/template, not only the six output scripts. Specify one central rule and test generated playthroughs to ensure each of the six Gatherings references it correctly.

### 5. Combat fiction and combat state diverge

Examples include surprise language that contradicts the initiative rule, a stated enemy count that does not match the combat unit, and fight defeat branches that do not continue to required discoveries. A fight that says “you are defeated; the story goes on” must actually have a route out of the fight which connects to the remaining chapter.

**Fix:** Make the combat state table the authoritative source for prose. For each fight define: number of opponents; who acts first and why; win state; defeat state; where the player is after either result; whether the fragment is accessible; and what persists into the next day.

### 6. Party size is a core feature, not a rare edge case

The game explicitly allows parties of 2–6, yet the scripts include a pair of named helpers, “four children” or four enemies in places where counts may read as literal, fixed role choices, hard-coded Arjun in the finale, and United Effort bars whose behaviour likely varies with number of participants.

**Fix:** Automated or manual route tests must include parties of 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6. Any use of a named friend should be a dynamic substitution from the actual party state. Any plural count should be classified as fixed fiction or party-scaled mechanics.

### 7. Campfires need fewer required prompts

The story wants friends to talk. The interface risks turning that into homework: a fragment meaning question, a theory question, each friend pinning a reading, a riddle with multiple tries, a weekly-answer line, an open question, and the read-back of notes. Repetition helps establish ritual, but mandatory repetition can reduce rather than increase conversation.

**Fix:** One prominent question per Campfire, one clue board, and no more than one optional extra (riddle or fragment interpretation) on most nights. Keep Garrick’s small acknowledgements and the extra cup. Let quiet nights be quiet.

### 8. The finale needs playable time, not merely readable time

The final Gathering asks players to vote, fight, coordinate a United Effort, read multiple revelations, match fragments, choose whether to give Garrick the fifth, respond to the second twist, decide an action in thirty seconds and pick a saved child. Its 20–30-minute target is not credible without evidence from a timed playtest.

**Fix:** Time the session with four ordinary players who have not seen the script. Record reading time, actual decision time, combat turns and discussion. Repeat with two and six players. Optimise for emotional room, not just for getting to the last line.

## The highest-priority repair list

Ranked by risk to game integrity and season payoff, not by how easy each change is.

### P0 — Fix before a real playtest

1. **Guarantee the full critical path.** All six weekly fragments, required locations, core revelations and next-step clues must remain available after Challenge failure, combat defeat and skipped Press On. This is a shared rule and a content rewrite, not a handful of local patches.
2. **Fix the map modifier in every Gathering.** Solving the map should not make the Approach harder unless that is explicitly intended and consistently explained.
3. **Complete every defeat branch.** State the immediate outcome, where the character ends up, what information they still receive, and what carries forward. The “story continues” rule must be true in the authored branches.
4. **Replace hard-coded people and party counts.** Test all five supported party sizes (2–6), especially in rescue beats, combat encounters, shared-effort turns and the finale’s one-heartbeat action.
5. **Set a single authoritative countdown and time line.** Reconcile Day 5’s two-night count, each dusk’s stroke, Day 6’s one remaining stroke, the full moon and the following dark moon. Every script card and recap must match it.

### P1 — Fix before ship

6. **Write a state-aware recap specification.** Recaps should reflect actual persistent events or explicitly report events observed by other friends. Never quietly assert that an optional branch happened.
7. **Define combat opponents and turn order precisely.** Each fight needs an unambiguous unit count, initiative source, success/failure result and end state. The Gathering’s Faithful/Hollowed assignment must be defined for all party sizes.
8. **Define United Effort mechanics completely.** Exact success/failure trigger for each bar, bar scaling by participant count, what a failure changes, and how simultaneous/edge outcomes resolve.
9. **Time the final Gathering and simplify its interaction load.** Preserve Garrick’s twist, but make room for the players to understand and react to it.
10. **Branch the finale’s transitions properly.** The “give the fifth/hold it back” vote and the “one heartbeat” action need a coherent setup whichever way the vote goes.

### P2 — Improve quality and trust

11. **Trim obligatory Campfire tasks.** Keep the communal question and clue board as the centre. Move riddles and secondary interpretations to optional extras or make some nights intentionally quiet.
12. **Audit Garrick’s truth-withholding lines for fairness.** Keep the narrow truths that gain a different meaning on reread. Ensure that enough clues exist before the finale that suspicion is possible, and that the after-finale recap explains rather than invents evidence.
13. **Standardise Pull/summary/calendar language.** “Tonight,” “tomorrow,” the Day 7 Gathering and the dawn opening need to agree everywhere.
14. **Review violence against the intended age and tone.** Decide what level of injury detail the game can support and apply it consistently.
15. **Run a name/prop/continuity pass.** Confirm the curved knife and its later references, the status of Wren/Joss after each day, which fragments are in the Chest, which clue was actually seen, what remains on the watch-page and which objects Garrick carries.

## Recommended test plan

Do not only read the happy path again. The bugs mostly exist in branch interactions.

### Route matrix

For each weekday, test at least these states:

- Main Challenge pass; Press On skipped.
- Main Challenge pass; Press On attempted and failed.
- Main Challenge fail.
- Combat victory.
- Combat defeat.
- Player misses yesterday and sees the recap.

Then compare the actual delivered facts and items with the next day’s recap, the fragment/map state and the next Gathering’s opening. Add extra routes anywhere the day contains a major choice or a persistent state change.

### Party-size matrix

Test parties of **2, 3, 4, 5 and 6**. The test should catch both fixed text and balance differences: who the script names, how many opponents appear, the number of turns before an effort bar reaches an end condition, how help calls work, and what happens if the randomly selected friend is defeated.

### Timed live test

Run Week 6’s Gathering with players who have not read the script. Track how long each sequence takes and ask them, after each major reveal, what they think just happened. If players are confused because the mystery is intentionally open, that can be good. If they are confused about whether a rule resolved or whether a clue was supposed to be known, that is not good mystery; it is interface debt.

### Reveal fairness test

Before showing the post-finale recap, ask the test group:

- When did you first suspect Garrick?
- Which specific lines made you suspicious?
- Did you believe he had lied, or did you believe he was withholding context?
- What did you think “moon-blood” meant before the finale?
- What clue proved the maps helped the Order locate the Stones?
- Did the reveal make earlier scenes more meaningful, or did it introduce explanations you could not have inferred?

Then compare their answers with the post-finale explanation. The goal is not that everyone solves the twist. The goal is that the twist feels inevitable in hindsight.

## Final recommendation

**Keep the six-week narrative and Garrick’s reveal. Do not ship the current branch logic.**

The story has a recognisable voice, a strong chain of places and clues, and an antagonist whose truthfulness becomes more frightening than a lie. The children matter, the extra cup matters, the maps matter, and the season ending makes the party’s success morally complicated without claiming that the rescues were meaningless.

But right now, required information is too often tied to optional success, combat victory or an intended route. Recaps then pretend the player followed that route. The Gathering has a systematic modifier that contradicts the value of the maps, the ending contains unscaled names and underdefined combat states, and the final session is too packed to trust its own duration promise.

The next iteration should be a **critical-path and state pass first, rules pass second, live pacing pass third**. Only after those are stable should the team spend serious time polishing individual lines. More prose is not the bottleneck. The bottleneck is making the game consistently deliver the story it has written.

*End of audit report.*
