A Challenge is a dice roll on one of your attributes: pass or fail changes gold, growth, and sometimes the scene, but not the plot. A Pull is the last line that says why tomorrow cannot wait. A colour choice is a button that is meant to change the feel of a moment without changing what happens. Last light (also called Fan Out) is the one optional clue after the chapter. A United Effort is one shared job the party takes in turns, with two bars: the obstacle, and the thing you might lose.

What a player believes by the end of the week: four strangers were handed their own names and sent to an inn; a missing scholar, Meira, wrote the list; quiet, wordless men are taking children on a calendar; you saved one girl, Tamsin; something was lifted out of an old mark before you arrived, and three other children's names are scratched under it. You do not yet know why your names were on the list. That last gap is fair. The rest is clear.

Day 1 — The forest road
A silent man cuts your axle, fights, and runs. He leaves a list with your name and an inn. The day's question is who he is and what he wants. You learn the second half only: he wanted you to find the list.

Story 8	A clean hook. Nothing is stolen; something is planted. The burned rubbing is a promise the week keeps.
Play 7	The fight and the box both belong in the scene. Win and loss are different to read, and then the same plot. The two "look" buttons are not a choice: you are told to open both.
Prose 9	The green light through the trees, the lock forced shut, the cold sliver. This is the voice the game should keep.
Pull 8	An inn, a day's walk, your name in a stranger's hand. I would open it.
What works. "Something has been put in." The robber who leaves a list is the right kind of wrong.

What does not.

Heading: Week 1, Day 1, Your thread. Quote: "He breaks off… he is gone" / "He could finish it… and then he is gone." Why: Pass and fail are both "he looks at you and leaves." Gold changes. The story does not. On the first day that teaches the player that fighting is a toll booth. Fix: On a win, he drops the burned rubbing and you see the second set of boots yourself. On a loss, you only find the rubbing later, in the box, and you do not know he had company. Size: Medium.
Heading: Week 1, Day 1, Your thread. Quote: "Four names. Yours is one of them." Why: The sample is written for four friends. Fix: Generate the count, and give every friend who is not "you" one small action this week (the way Mei, Dev and Arjun are used), or the missing friends become furniture. Size: Big, but it is a season rule, not a Day 1 rewrite.

Day 4 — Fenmark

Story 9	The week's heart. The warm bowl, the counting, the elder's hands.
Play 7	Pass or fail changes whether the boy is standing beside you when the men arrive. That matters. The three talk-choices and the three stance-choices are then followed by one shared paragraph.
Prose 9	"He is not hiding what happened here. He is terrified of what happens to this village if it is known that somebody talked."
Pull 9	He has half a day on you, and he is running toward the only other witness.

What does not.

Heading: Week 1, Day 4, Your thread. Quote: "[SHOW HIM THE LIST] or [ASK WHO WAS TAKEN] or [SAY NOTHING. WAIT.] … the story is the same." Wait, and he starts talking because you did not. Stance: if you step in front of him, the elder looks at you, not at the boy. Size: Medium.

Day 5 — The old charcoal camp

Story 9	The calendar turn is clean, and the text lets you count it before it announces it.
Play 7	The look at the path is the only branch that tells you he is here, and it is also the branch that lets him strike first. Interesting, easy to feel cheated by.

Pull 8	"If it comes round like a calendar, the time is now." Strong, but you already know you are going north. Slightly less urgent than Fenmark.

What does not.

Match, Ask, and the long scripture are absent. Good. Night one should not teach every device. The question to the table is the right difficulty: children's names, with a hint if the chat is quiet.

The week
Story. It builds in a straight line: list, author, empty rock, named child, a previous child, a map, a rescue that is not the point. No contradiction. The linen and the courier are plants, not holes. The nephew who was never found is a question left open on purpose; say so in the season summary later if he never returns, or the player will wait for him.

Play. The weekday shape teaches itself in the right order: fight, Challenge, Press On, fragment, one lead, fire, then on Day 7 a vote, a shared fight, a private notice, and one shared job. Choices that change a roll (Day 3, Day 5, the Approach) feel better than choices that change nothing (Day 4). Gold is present and not yet interesting. The Store is barely mentioned. That is fine for week one.

Campfire. It is one form too many from Day 2 on: clue board, fragment question, tonight's question, riddle, a line for the case, an open question, a bottle. The aim is talk. The risk is homework. I would keep the board, one pin, and the riddle. Fold the fragment's "what does this mean?" into tonight's question whenever they are really the same conversation. Make the riddle easy to skip without a guilty button. Day 1, with no Garrick and no riddle, is the right weight. Do not add that weight back.

Compared with a table like D&D. Players cannot do the unexpected, and the story will not bend. For a six-week chat game with stored text, that is the right trade, as long as the buttons you do offer are honest. The fire is where freedom has to live: a pin, a sentence, a bottle. If those are multiple-choice with a blank, friends will still talk in their own chat. They will not talk inside the forms. Do not add more forms to make it feel more like a table.

Garrick. Likeable. Specific. Funny without performing. He never promises and never predicts in this week. "Family habit. We always pour one for whoever can't be with us" sounds like a person. The risk of the computer voice is not his scene lines. It is the kit that repeats every night: "You're first. Pull up a log," four goodnights, and "I saw." Those will harden by Week 4 if they do not vary with what actually happened. His morning notes are some of the best prose in the week. Protect them.

Would I log in? Yes. Fenmark, the horse, the map, and Tamsin are enough. I might rush Day 3, because I have already fought this man.

A friend who misses a day can be told the recap. They cannot be told "nobody missed a fragment" if they did. Catch-up has to be a rule the prose obeys.

Party of two. The United Effort scales its bars down, which is good. The prose still needs a second body in the hole and someone to hold ankles. Write the alone version as carefully as you wrote Dev's, every time.

Week score: 8 / 10. A strong first week, with a voice worth keeping and a Gathering that pays off the rescue while withholding the real answer. The holes are repetition, a few dead buttons, and a Campfire that asks the wrong question out loud.

Three fixes, in order:

Make every fire question the same question as its pins. Cut the fragment pin on nights where tonight's question already covers it.
Stop ending every road fight with a silent escape. Day 1 and Day 3 need a different leftover.
Let the Gathering answer one true piece of "why were our names on that list?" without explaining the mark.
