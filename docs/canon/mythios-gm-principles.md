# Mythios — Game-Master Principles

**What this document is:** a universal rulebook for building any Mythios season, extracted from the design process of *Tales of the Dark Moon*. This is not story-specific — it contains no Malakor, no Garrick, no Stones. It is meant to be readable and usable by an AI instance (or a human designer) with zero prior context, generating a brand-new story in any setting.

**Why this document exists:** an AI can already generate a passable mystery plot on request. What it cannot do without explicit instruction is reliably avoid the specific, repeatable failure modes that turn a clever plot into a confusing one, or a good twist into an unbelievable one. This document is the accumulated set of those failure modes and their fixes, stated as reusable principles — not tied to any one story.

**This is a living document.** New principles get added here as they're discovered in future design sessions, on any story.

---

## 1. Structural Principles

**1.0 — Terminology, disambiguated (established after finding real overlap in practice).** These four terms are related but distinct, and should not be used interchangeably:
- **Debunk** — an episode's investigation-level answer, stated analytically (e.g., "not a random attack — six were chosen").
- **Twist** — the *dramatic delivery* of that same debunk, always at the Gathering. Same content as the Debunk, different mode.
- **Reveal** — reserved specifically for the named "Lore Reveal" Gathering beat (world-building/history information). Do not use generically for "any revelation" — say "revelation" or "payoff" instead, to avoid confusion with this specific beat.
- **Layer** — a season-*wide*, multi-episode thread (Layer A, B, C...), never a single-episode concept. Distinct in scope from all three terms above, which are episode- or Gathering-scoped.

**1.1 — A-plot/B-plot discipline.** Every episode needs a self-contained investigation that resolves on its own, *and* must advance a season-long arc. Neither alone is sufficient — a purely episodic structure has no momentum across weeks; a purely serialized structure gives no satisfying weekly payoff.

**1.2 — Twists must reframe, never merely replace.** A season built as "question → answer → new unrelated question" (a chain of trapdoors) fails to give the audience a growing sense of understanding. Every twist should make the audience feel *"I understand the shape of this now"* — not *"okay, apparently that wasn't it either, what next."* Challenge: does this episode's answer change what an earlier answer *meant*, or does it just introduce a fresh, disconnected mystery?

**1.3 — Antagonist pressure must stay active throughout.** Long stretches where the antagonist is purely historical or passive (being investigated, rather than actively opposing) will read as "the plot is waiting for me" in an async format, where players already supply their own momentum by choosing to log in. Every episode should have some form of active opposition, even subtle.

**1.4 — Front-loading protects short sessions.** In any format where a participant might engage for a short or long duration in a single sitting, all essential/mandatory content must be deliverable in the short-duration case. Optional content extends for longer engagement but must never carry anything load-bearing. Test: could someone who only reads the first fixed block still follow the whole season if they read only that block every time?

**1.5 — Live/synchronous sessions need their own reserved climax.** If a format has both asynchronous individual content and a periodic live/shared session, do not let the front-loading rule (1.4) accidentally push a week's big reveal into the solo portion before the group ever assembles. Reserve the actual reveal for the shared session; the solo portion leading into it should build tension and stakes without resolving them.

**1.6 — Default shared-session shape: Battle → Lore Reveal (if due) → Twist → Cliffhanger.** For a climactic shared/live session, deliver the tangible payoff (combat, a physical confrontation) first, then optional world-building context, then the informational Twist that reframes it, then a forward-pulling Cliffhanger — as four distinct beats, never blended. Blending them causes each to undercut the other. **The two rules beats sit around this spine: the Approach before the Battle and the United Effort after it (Core Rules §1), so the full order is Approach → Battle → United Effort → Lore Reveal (if due) → Twist → Cliffhanger.** *(Corrected from an earlier 3-beat version of this principle — Action → Revelation → Cliffhanger — which conflated Lore Reveal and Twist into one beat; see 1.0 for why these are now kept distinct.)*

**1.7 — Calendar-aligned pacing eases continuity.** If a season runs across real calendar time, mapping "story day" to "real day" (rather than letting story-time run independent of real-time) makes recaps, notifications, and re-engagement mechanics dramatically simpler to reason about and build.

**1.8 — The Daily Beat Template, corrected.** An earlier version of this principle treated "what's found" and "which Layer advances" as two separate, parallel facts about a day — this was a real structural error, not just a phrasing issue. **A day's discovery and its Layer progress are almost always the same event, described at two different zoom levels — the local story detail, and what it means season-wide.** They should be written as one fused statement, never split into two lines pretending to be independent. Corrected template, four points:
1. **Location** — stated plainly on arrival, never assumed from a bare name.
2. **Found — one fused fact: the concrete story detail *and* which season Layer(s) it's the vehicle for, stated together.** Earned through a specific action (a Challenge, a fight, a choice) — never pure coincidence. **If this fact doesn't serve any Layer at all, that's not a neutral "connective tissue" day — it's the filler-day problem, and the day needs a different discovery or should be cut.** (This replaces an earlier, incorrect allowance for "connective tissue" days with no Layer work — testing revealed every currently-working day in this season's actual content serves at least one Layer; a day that serves none has no real value.)
3. **The Pull** — where next, and why it can't wait. Practical, logistical framing.
4. **The Lure** — *(retired 5 Oct 2026 with all optional play; a chapter now ends on the Pull and the day summary card. Kept here for the history.)* Optional, non-load-bearing hook. Either fully isolated, or a deliberate Recurring Lure per 2.7 — never an unflagged accidental connection.

*(A story-specific Layer table — e.g. this season's Layer A through G — belongs in that story's own content file, not here; this principle only states the general rule that daily discovery and Layer progress must be written as one fact, never two.)*

**1.8a — A Layer reference document is mandatory before any Layer is cited, and citation format is non-negotiable.** Every Layer mention, anywhere — an episode file, a summary table, casual conversation — must use the full form `Layer [Letter] ([Full Name])`, never a bare letter alone. This rule has been dropped in practice more than once, specifically when working from memory instead of the actual reference document — treat that as confirmation the rule needs the document, not just good intentions. If the reference document isn't directly visible while writing, re-read it first.

**1.8b — A day's Pull names tomorrow's location; the actual travel is connective tissue, not tomorrow's opening.** The next day should open with the party already *at* the new location, its payoff beginning immediately — never with more journey narration first. Any transition, travel, or en-route content belongs to the *current* day (as texture following the Pull, or as a Lure), not as a delayed preamble to the next one. Found and corrected in Episode 1, where an early draft of Day 7 opened by re-narrating a map's completion that had already happened at the end of Day 6 — the fix was to open Day 7 already arrived, not repeat the transition.

**1.9 — The Gathering Template — related to 1.8, not identical, corrected the same way.** A Gathering cannot use the daily template directly: it has no Lure, its existing named four-beat shape (1.6) should not be replaced, and its forward-hook (Cliffhanger) is emotional/dramatic rather than the Pull's practical framing. Same fusion principle as 1.8 applies — Twist and Layer progress are one fact, not two:
1. **Location** — same standard as daily beats.
2. **Battle payoff** — what's earned/rescued through the fight, fused with whichever Layer it serves, if any — same "no accidental payoff" standard as daily point 2.
3. **Lore Reveal** — only present if genuinely due this week; skipping it is correct, not a gap, when there's no new world-lore to deliver. Genuinely distinct from Twist, not fused with it.
4. **Twist** — the week's Debunk, delivered dramatically, stated as one fused fact with the season Layer rung it lands — not restated separately as "Layer work." Always present.
5. **Cliffhanger** — the emotional/dramatic forward hook. Not a Pull; should never read as a practical travel instruction.

**3a. The Connection — a standing part of every Lore Reveal** *(added 2026-09-27; TVMen: "we should make this a feature in the gathering")*. Straight after each part of a found document is read, lay the week's matching fragments (and any Meira note) beside the lines they match. Quote each one whole, say where it was found, and give it one plain sentence saying what it now means. Then say outright, in a sentence or two, what the party now knows — and name what is still unanswered. Never leave players to assemble the connection unaided: clever is fine, unclear is not. A long document can be read in parts, with a Connection after each part.

**1.10 — Cliffhanger types: three, not an open list. The strongest cliffhangers combine magnitude and interruption in the same breath — never a calm reveal followed by a separate moment of danger.**
- **Mid-Action Chaos (the default favorite):** the interruption itself is where the drama lives — a real event breaking in mid-scene (a wall giving way, an enemy bursting in mid-sentence). Strongest because its resolution naturally satisfies Immediate Continuation (1.11) — the very next instant *is* the payoff.
- **Interrupted Reveal:** a massive, plot-defining fact delivered right as it becomes visible, cut before reaction lands. **The interruption doesn't require a literal mid-sentence cut** — a *complete* statement can be equally interrupted if what's withheld is the follow-up: mechanism, cost, or truthfulness. Test: does the statement open an urgent "how / at what price / is this even true" gap, not just curiosity about meaning? (Strongest known example: a constrained-honesty antagonist delivering a technically-true statement that reads as salvation to the party and reads very differently to a rereading, suspicious audience — the mechanism and the doubt arrive in the same line.)
- **Named Clock (occasional, structurally different — not interchangeable with the other two):** a real deadline, cut at the moment it's set, not after the party's already reacting to it. **Cannot resolve via Immediate Continuation's usual shape** (a few lines then pivot) — a clock's tension only works if it's genuinely shown ticking across multiple beats. When used, the clock becomes the spine of the *entire following episode* (each day visibly eating into the remaining time, a worsening complication daily), not a device resolved in an opening beat. Use sparingly, and only when built for this heavier shape.

**1.11 — Immediate Continuation.** Next episode's Day 1 opens by resolving the *exact* interrupted moment in its first lines — never a time-skip, never "meanwhile," never a fresh scene. Only after that beat resolves decisively does the day pivot hard into new momentum. Applies literally to Mid-Action Chaos (the interrupted action plays out) and Interrupted Reveal (the party's immediate reaction, shown at once) — Named Clock is the deliberate exception (1.10).

**1.12 — Failure should cost gold, and the story should still move.** A Challenge never blocks the story. A failed Challenge costs gold (−150g, Core Rules §3), and its failure branch should still be vivid, with a visible consequence in the scene. It carries nothing into the Gathering Battle. **A failure must still look like it had a stake (TVMen, 2026-10-03):** the player should see a real cost or setback in the scene (lost time, a rougher road, a harder conversation) even when the plot's way forward is quietly kept open behind the scenes. Whether the detour is pulled back to the main path is a backend decision, never shown as "you couldn't have lost". *(Added 2026-09-29, series audit item 4, as "carried costs" (a wound, being seen). Balance-tested 2026-09-30. **Retired 2026-10-03 (TVMen: "failed check lose gold and not HP", then "gold only").** The history: "one extra enemy" was tried first, but cut a 2-player party's win chance from 89% to 40%; "the Order strikes first" replaced it; both were then removed.)*

**1.13 — Vary the weekly shape.** A repeated formula lets players predict the week. Keep the fixed spine (the Order always reaches the socket first), but vary what surrounds it: escalate the near-miss (long gone → dust barely disturbed → a glimpse of it leaving → dust still falling), vary how found documents arrive (dropped, read aloud by an enemy, recited as a dying prayer), and don't assemble every map the same way. *(Added 2026-09-29, series audit item 4.)*

**1.14 — Roll only when it can fail and something is at stake.** A long session is mostly Choices and Talk, which are free. Offer a Challenge card only where there is a real reason the attempt could fail and something to win or lose. A player's first Challenge of the day is their Daily Challenge (gold and growth); every later one is a Bonus Challenge (the one exception is the safety net, Core Rules §3), whose only stake is a non-essential extra that a failure really shuts off. Neither Talk nor a Bonus Challenge may ever carry plot. The Lure is the natural doorway into Bonus Challenges. **MYTHIOS never invents a roll: a Challenge card or a fight comes only from the script (TVMen, 2026-10-03, after Audit E).** *(Added 2026-10-03; Core Rules §3, "The four kinds of moment".)*

**1.15 — Tell the story to each player; let the party chat be where they meet; play together at the Gathering.** In a game played at different hours, anything one player must do before the others can read on will stall the group. So each player gets the day's beat and rolls its Challenge in their own thread, and the party chat carries one-line results, the travelling companion's voice and the friends' own talk. Save the shared, live play for the Gathering, and give it shared things to do: an Approach (a group Choice and a volunteer's Challenge) before the Battle, and one United Effort after it, so the long read that follows is earned. Helping a friend always costs a Relic. *(Added 2026-10-03; Core Rules §1, "The three places", and §5.)*

**1.16 — The road always goes on.** An improvising GM will be tested: a player will attack the guide, refuse the journey, or demand the ending. Answer inside the story, and return to the day's Pull. Characters a later scene needs cannot be killed, captured or driven off outside a scripted beat; plot is delivered only in scripted beats; whatever the GM invents in free play is small, local and disposable. *(Added 2026-10-03; Core Rules §11, "The GM's limits".)*

## 2. Clue and Fair-Play Principles



**2.1 — Mystery lives in meaning withheld, never facts withheld.** The audience should always know a relevant object, detail, or event *exists*, even if they don't yet know what it *means*. Introducing something as pure unexplained atmosphere and only later revealing it existed at all is not a twist, it's an ambush.

**2.2 — No implied visuals in text formats.** A visual medium can let an audience notice a detail themselves via framing, lingering, or juxtaposition. Text has no equivalent — if a detail is load-bearing, it must be stated directly in prose. Never assume a reader will infer significance from an undescribed image.

**2.3 — The setup can be subtle; the reveal must be unmissable.** Restraint and ambiguity belong in the planting of a clue, not in the delivery of its payoff. Once a twist actually lands, state it plainly, and consider restating it a second way in the same scene. A clever reveal that's also cryptic risks the whole subsequent plot being lost on the audience.

**2.4 — Any clue supporting a major twist needs 5+ occurrences, not 2-3.** A clue that appears only two or three times, however clearly each instance is written, risks being missed by anyone who engages inconsistently with the format. Count actual occurrences before considering a twist adequately planted. Repeat as ambient, recurring texture — not as isolated "special" moments only.

**2.5 — Planted clues must survive summary/recap compression.** If the format includes any kind of "catch-up" or recap mechanism for those who missed content, a clue that only exists in the full, un-summarized version is invisible to anyone relying on the recap. Explicitly tag load-bearing clues so any summarization process (human or AI) is forced to preserve them, rather than trusting a summarizer's judgment about what's "important."

**2.6 — Distinguish "the setup is intriguing" from "the reveal is convoluted."** When building toward a payoff, periodically stress-test by writing the actual reveal scene early. If explaining the reveal requires more than a few sentences of new information not already hinted at, the setup hasn't done enough work — add more plants rather than making the reveal scene do all the explaining at once.

**2.7 — A Lure may echo a previous Lure, provided neither ever becomes load-bearing.** The default rule (a Lure is optional, non-load-bearing, and never referenced again) exists to keep optional content genuinely safe to skip. But two Lures that quietly rhyme with each other — the same small object recurring, an unexplained detail answered by another unexplained detail — don't violate that safety on their own; they add atmosphere for an attentive player without ever penalizing an inattentive one. **The test: could the second instance be cut, or the first be missed entirely, with zero effect on any plot thread, twist, or `[PLANT]` clue chain?** If yes, the echo is safe — treat it as texture, not plot. If no — if noticing the connection unlocks or foreshadows anything mechanically or narratively real — it has actually become a `[PLANT]` in disguise, and needs the full clue treatment (5+ occurrences, explicit tagging, recap-survival) instead of living as an unflagged "optional" thread. **First identified use case:** two matching carved tokens, left by an unexplained party across consecutive days in early testing — cutting either token, or a player missing one entirely, changes nothing about the season's actual mystery. *(Update 2026-09-28: this season's tokens were cut anyway, under 2.11 — an optional mystery that never gets an answer is safe, but it still costs attention the twists need.)*

**2.8 — Audit every found document against every later misreading it has to survive.** When a season's twists depend on the audience *misreading* a document later, any earlier document that states the true answer — even in passing, even in a line written for a different purpose — quietly makes the later misreading impossible. Check each in-world text forward, not just backward: for every later twist that relies on a wrong belief, ask whether anything already in the audience's hands contradicts that belief. **First identified use case:** a torn page meant to seed a "the fifth piece will stop it" misreading also said, two lines earlier, exactly whose blood the fifth piece needed — which would have made the next episode's misreading unbelievable. In the same pass, a line meant to support the misreading contradicted a rule the audience had already been shown ("each piece needs one specific person" vs. an earlier "anyone of the right kind will do"). Both passed a backward check (consistent with what came before) and failed the forward one.

**2.9 — Player-facing narration never winks at the deceiver, and never plays a live scene's choices for the players.** Two separate leaks, found together. **(a) The wink:** a narrator line like "True, and warmly meant — which is exactly what makes it work" tells players the character is performing, which gives away the long con in the very line meant to hide it. Explanations of *why* a deceptive line works belong in GM notes and design documents, never in text read aloud. The narrator also mustn't state a character's inner state as fact when it's false ("he isn't holding anything back"). Describe what he looks like, not what he is. **(b) The scripted choice:** in a live, shared scene, narration that says "you hand him the stone" or "nobody presses" decides for the players. Every load-bearing choice in a live scene needs a branch for the other answer, and the likeliest questions need scripted, true answers ready. **First identified use case:** a season's climax scenes, where an audit found both leaks in the same pass.

**2.10 — Every found fragment must be important setup or drama — nothing else earns the slot.** A found document delivered in pieces (here, the Seeker Fragments) exists to make the lore understandable and clear in hindsight. So each piece is either **setup** — an important piece of the lore, planted before the reveal that explains it, so the reveal clicks when it's quoted back — or **drama**, a vivid, emotional moment from the lore in the writer's own voice. A **seed** that pays off episodes later is allowed only if it carries a finale thread **and** reads plainly once its payoff arrives. Colour that is neither (a charming detail, a cryptic line whose meaning is never actually stated) doesn't earn a fragment — it belongs in the longer in-world documents instead. Test each one: when it's quoted back at its payoff, does the reader instantly see what it meant and why it mattered? *(Added 2026-09-27; TVMen: "fragments should help to setup the lore to make the lore understandable and clear on hindsight. and naturally, it should take either important setup bits, or dramatic bits from the lore.")*

**2.11 — Keep the twist clever; keep everything else simple.** A twist only lands if players can hold the rest of the story in their heads, and every extra rule, tier, exception or number competes with it for room. So around each twist: one simple rule beats two nuanced ones; the stakes are stated plainly (what happens if the enemy wins is never vague); and every setup is clear enough that, quoted back at the payoff, it clicks at once. Test: can the episode's situation be explained in two sentences with no "except" or "but only"? *(Added 2026-09-28. TVMen: "as much as we want reveal and twist, and perhaps because of it, we should make (other) things simple and setup clearly… keep the twist clever and yet clear and obvious on hindsight." First cases: the faithful were split into two tiers of knowledge — cut to one rule; and Episode 3's stakes were left abstract — the corruption now lands there plainly.)*

## 3. Consistency and Clarity Principles

**3.1 — Fixed quantities need an explicit arithmetic check, immediately.** Any time a story defines a fixed number of anything (magic items, factions, chosen people, key locations), check that number against every other fixed number it touches, the moment it's introduced — not later, by accident, when generating specific instances. A "5 heroes, 5 weapons, but 6 chosen descendants" mismatch can sit undetected through extensive design work if nobody ever does the actual count.

**3.2 — No shared root words between distinct proper nouns.** Names that sound similar (e.g., a location and a nearby but different-purpose object sharing a root) will blur together in the mind of someone engaging inconsistently with the material. Audit all names in a season together, not just individually as they're created.

**3.3 — State a location or object's type on first mention.** Don't assume a name alone conveys what kind of thing it refers to. State it plainly the first time ("a village," "a shrine," "an old mining settlement") so a reader who encounters the bare name later already knows its category.

**3.4 — Watch for word collisions with existing significant terms.** If a word already carries specific mechanical or narrative weight in the story (e.g., a term reserved for a major macguffin), avoid reusing that same word for an unrelated, mundane object — even in passing, even in throwaway description. The collision risks implying a connection that doesn't exist.

**3.5 — A new rule or change is not verified until it's checked against every constraint already governing that element, not just the one rule you were actively applying.** This failure happened in layers across one project's design process, each worse than the last: (1) a location named too early was caught once via a narrow check, then found again in ordinary dialogue the same narrow check had skipped; (2) a fix for "objects must be physically portable" was made without checking it against "these objects must also plausibly survive centuries," a constraint stated explicitly earlier in the *same conversation* — meaning the failure wasn't a missing check, it was ignoring an answer already given; (3) a claim that a story event counted as progress on a season-long thread was made without tracing its *downstream consequence* — if true, it would have made a separate, already-locked milestone later in the season redundant, since the party would have already known by then what that milestone was supposed to newly reveal. **"I verified this against the rule I was fixing" is not the same claim as "I verified this against everything else established about this element," and only the second one is real verification.** Before presenting any fix as settled: (a) run a full-file search for the changed term(s), not a scoped one; (b) explicitly re-list every constraint already established for that specific element — materials, timelines, prior stated reasoning — and check the fix against all of them together, not just the most recent one; (c) check the fix for repetition against its own category (other instances of the same *kind* of thing — other hiding spots, other names, other creatures), not just against contradiction; (d) trace the new claim's logical consequences forward — if this is true, does it make anything *else* already established redundant, impossible, or nonsensical, even if nothing directly contradicts it in text.

**3.6 — Decisions made in conversation are not decisions made in the document.** When refining a story collaboratively over an extended conversation, explicitly verify that each agreed-upon fix has actually been applied to the saved material — do not assume alignment between "what was discussed" and "what was written." Run periodic, explicit audits of the actual files against the actual decisions.

**3.7 — A "comprehensive audit" that skips any file, or only spot-checks instead of fully reading, is not comprehensive — and any name or term reused across a session needs the same verification as a new one, precisely because familiarity is what lets errors like this survive.** A prior audit this session was presented as comprehensive but never fully read one tracker file and never opened one episode file's own content — the exact place a three-way name conflict (filename vs. H1 header vs. Deception Table title) had already been sitting, undetected, for the rest of the session. **The concrete, mechanical fix: for any set of names/titles/terms that should match across multiple files (episode titles, character names, mechanic names), run a direct extraction-and-compare pass** — pull the term as it appears in every file that references it, list them side by side, and look for disagreement directly, rather than trusting recall or a partial read. This check takes seconds and catches what memory-based review reliably misses. Demonstrated working: a single grep-based extraction across episode titles, tracker references, file headers, and filenames immediately surfaced a conflict that had been undetected through an entire prior "comprehensive" pass.

**3.7a — "Check," "audit," or "comprehensive" always includes the full session transcripts, not just the current working files — no exception, no judgment call about whether it seems likely to matter.** This was a real, separate failure from 3.7: the actual project transcripts (in `/mnt/transcripts/`) were never grepped during any "comprehensive" pass this session, even though they were directly accessible and grep-able in seconds — the elements-naming answer was sitting there, findable immediately once actually searched, after multiple failed attempts that only checked current `.md` files and other past chats. **The pattern was treating current working files as "the real project" and transcripts as background not worth checking unless something specifically pointed there — that framing is wrong and is retired as of this rule.** Going forward, any request to check, verify, or audit something automatically includes a grep pass across every transcript file, every time, as a standing default — not something to remember to do, something that's simply always part of what "check" means.

**3.8 — Enemy counts scale with the party.** Every Gathering fight is a private one-on-one per player (Core Rules), so a scripted fight has exactly [N] opponents, one for each player — never a fixed number. Anything that depends on the count (a prisoner asked how many came, a body count) uses [N] too. Describing a crowd is fine as atmosphere, but the combat line still says each player faces their own opponent. *(Added 2026-09-27: the Ep2 Gathering said "six robed figures", and the Ep3 one had enemies fighting "in pairs" and a prisoner answering "Seven.")*

**3.9 — Don't number what players could confuse with their own party, and keep numbered things to a minimum.** When party size varies, a named group with a fixed count ("the Six") invites players to map it onto themselves, and every extra number is one more thing to track beside the ones that matter (here, four Stones and a fifth). Name groups by what they are ("the Seekers"), and say a count only where the count itself is the clue. *(Added 2026-09-28; TVMen: "can we not use the Six and just call them seekers… we have a complexity of 4 and 5 stones to deal with (remember also, player number is varied).")*

**3.7b — A direct statement from the user takes precedence immediately, on the first instance — not after a second or third repetition.** Corrected after an initial version of this rule set the bar too late: the user's own instruction was explicit — their word is authoritative right away unless *they themselves* frame it as a suggestion (language like "what if," "maybe," "should we consider"), in which case it's genuinely open for evaluation and pushback. A distinct, opposite failure from 3.5/3.7 above: those are about checking thoroughly before accepting a new claim. This is about a direct statement that should never have been re-litigated at all — a user stated "Episode 1 finds the 1st stone," and it was treated as a fresh proposal to verify against old file content (a Deception Table letter implying otherwise) rather than accepted immediately. **The correct sequence: accept the direct statement as ground truth first, then check the rest of the project for anything that contradicts it, and flag whatever's found — never use a found contradiction to cast doubt on or relitigate what the user actually said.** Checking still happens, and still matters — it just runs in service of finding what needs to change, not in service of deciding whether the user was right.

## 4. Antagonist Design Principles

**4.1 — A constrained antagonist (e.g., "cannot lie") needs a grounded, in-story reason for the constraint.** An arbitrary authorial rule imposed on a character for the writer's convenience will not survive improvisation and doesn't function as real characterization. Give the constraint an origin within the world's own logic (a code, a doctrine, a superstition) so it's something the character genuinely believes and practices, not a magic trick.

**4.2 — Design constrained antagonists for an *aspirational*, not absolute, standard if the delivery system may improvise.** If an AI or human GM will be generating some of the antagonist's dialogue live, "he never lies, ever, with zero exceptions" is fragile — a single accidental slip breaks the premise. "He strives never to lie, and is unnervingly good at it" survives an imperfect delivery and is arguably better characterization (a practiced discipline, not a magical guarantee). **In Tales of the Dark Moon the rule is absolute, not aspirational: Garrick never lies. That is safe because every line of his that touches the plot is scripted, and outside the script he may only make small talk or turn a question back (Core Rules §11).**

**4.3 — Distinguish scripted lines from safe-to-improvise lines.** Explicitly mark which of an antagonist's lines are load-bearing (must be delivered close to verbatim, because their exact phrasing does specific work — double meanings, fair-play clues) versus which are incidental (safe for live variation). Do not leave this distinction implicit.

**4.4 — A long-con antagonist should be shown to actively steer events, not just passively avoid suspicion.** "He was secretly evil the whole time" lands as a cheap twist if his only prior behavior was being nice and not getting caught. "He was actively making decisions happen, and every one of them benefited him" is a stronger reveal — plan concrete moments across the season where his suggestion or subtle nudge caused something, not just where he happened to have information.

**4.5 — Build a "deception table" as a living design tool, not just a delivery script.** For any antagonist whose whole arc depends on deception holding up under scrutiny, maintain a document tracking every one of their statements alongside its literal truth and its true meaning. Writing this document *is* the audit — it will surface impossible or contradictory lines (e.g., an antagonist who "never lies" being asked to forge evidence) before they're locked into the story.

**4.6 — For a "constrained honesty" antagonist, name the specific verbs where accidental lies actually live.** In practice, a character who "cannot lie" breaks the constraint almost exclusively through three verb categories: **predicting** an outcome ("we'll stop this in time"), **promising** something ("I'll get you there"), or **denying knowledge** ("I've no idea"). All three claim certainty about the future or about the character's own mind — exactly where a hidden agenda actually lives. Naming these three explicitly, as a checklist, turns an abstract constraint into something an AI GM (or any improviser) can reliably self-check line by line, rather than trying to verify each new sentence against everything the character secretly knows. A safe, general-purpose deflection template that satisfies the constraint against nearly any question: *"I won't promise you that."* *(Changed 2026-09-28 from "I'd rather not promise you a thing I can't see the end of": a character who knows the end claiming he can't see it is fake doubt — a fourth place accidental lies live. Declining is enough.)*

**4.7 — Every authored line for a constrained-honesty antagonist should pass three tests.** (1) Is it **literally true**? (2) Does it read **innocently** on first pass and **differently** on reread, once the audience knows what the character actually is? (3) Could the character plausibly know this **only as far ahead as their actual information allows** — never earlier than the audience themselves could reason it out? A line that fails any of these needs rewriting before it's locked into a deception table or delivered live.

## 5. Motivation and Character Principles

**5.1 — A sympathetic, wronged character used purely as a plot-delivery device weakens on scrutiny.** If a character exists mainly to appear, dispense necessary information, and disappear, an attentive audience will eventually notice the pattern and stop treating their appearances as organic. Give such a character their own independent goal, something they want that isn't simply "help the protagonists," even if that goal is revealed gradually or only after their death/absence.

**5.2 — Sustained personal stakes should not decay into "because the plot says so."** Opening hooks are often personal and visceral; investigation-heavy middle content risks becoming procedural and externally-motivated. Periodically check: does the protagonist still have a reason to care that's about *them*, specifically, not just about solving an abstract mystery?

**5.3 — A betrayer has to be liked before he can betray.** A traitor who is only useful (a guide, a map-reader) is furniture, and nobody grieves furniture. Give him warmth players feel week after week: he notices people, looks after them without fuss, and has a dry, understated wit. Warmth, not jokes, is the goal. Most of it lives in the live chat, with a few scripted running bits (a habit, a kindness, one quiet confession) that read as affection the first time and as the betrayal on reread. When the reveal lands, take the warmth away completely; its absence is part of the gut punch. For Garrick specifically, see the Deception Table's "Garrick's Voice" section. *(Added 2026-09-29, TVMen: "not haha humourous, but definitely likable".)*

## 6. Production and Delivery Principles

**6.1 — Match narrative content length to its actual narrative importance, not to a rigid per-scene template.** A climactic reveal scene may warrant significantly more length/detail than a routine daily beat — don't compress a major reveal to fit a size that was calibrated for lower-stakes content, and don't pad routine content to match the reveal's scale either.

**6.2 — Punctuate long reveal scenes with interactive beats.** A long stretch of pure narration risks reading as an unbroken wall of text. Insert small interactive challenges (a roll, a choice) at natural points within a longer scene to break up pacing and keep engagement active throughout, not just at the scene's start and end.

**6.3 — Test whether a "test/comparison" device accidentally reveals more than intended.** A common way to demonstrate a character's unique ability is to have another character attempt and fail at the same task. Audit this carefully — the *specifics* of what the failing character says or knows can leak information about the mystery object faster than intended. If the comparison device keeps forcing premature reveals, abandon it in favor of demonstrating the ability through the protagonists' own self-observation instead.

**6.5 — Player-facing writing must be a joy to read — vivid, never convoluted.** Paint the place and the world: a concrete image, a human detail, a line with rhythm. But every sentence has to be followable on one reading aloud. When the two pull against each other, clarity wins — the image gets simpler, not cut. Found documents (scripture, fragments) get the same care: a real story shape — a hook, rising stakes, a turn, an ending — never a summary. *(Added 2026-09-27; TVMen: "keep the writing interesting (a joy to read, paint the scene & world making, but not too convoluted)".)*

**6.6 — Name every found document by what it is, every time.** When a scene holds more than one document, never call one "it" or "the book" — use its name ("the ledger", "the Order's scripture"). On first appearance, say plainly what kind of document it is, what it looks like, whose it is, and how it differs from the ones players already carry (Seeker fragments, Meira's notes). Design-document names stay out of narration: players hear "the Order's scripture", never "Cult Bible". *(Added 2026-09-27, after the Ep2 Gathering's "Garrick reads it" and "He closes the book" left it unclear which of two books was meant.)*

**6.4 — Log terminology in one place and reuse it exactly.** Once a mechanic, sensation, or concept has an established name or description, use the *same* wording every time it recurs, rather than re-describing it differently each time. Consistent repetition is what allows an inconsistent audience to recognize a recurring thread.
