## Season-Wide Authoring Principles

Two standing rules, established while writing Episode 1's daily beats, that apply to every episode going forward.

**1. Mystery lives in meaning withheld, never in facts withheld.**

The party may not know what something *means* yet, but they must always know that it *exists*, plainly stated. Example: the symbol on the Day 1 parchment scraps was initially left ambiguous — a mistake. Fixed by stating outright, on first appearance, that it is a **rubbing** (charcoal worked over something hard and raised, not drawn or written). That one concrete fact is what lets a later reveal ("this is a rubbing of the Stone that sat in this socket") feel earned rather than sprung. If a detail is meant to pay off later, it must be introduced as a plain, memorable fact at first appearance — never as pure unexplained atmosphere.

**2. This is text, not a visual medium — nothing can be implied through imagery alone.**

A camera can linger on an object and let an audience notice something themselves. Text has no equivalent; there is no close-up for personal discernment. **If a detail is load-bearing, it must be stated directly in prose.** Never write an ambiguous description and expect a reader to catch significance that was never actually said. This is a stricter standard than most visual fiction requires, and it applies to every object, symbol, and behavioral tic in the Garrick Deception Table as much as to plot clues.



**3. The setup can be subtle; the reveal must be unmissable.**

Daily plants stay ambiguous and deniable on purpose -- that is where mystery lives, and where the season's clever misdirection belongs. But once a twist actually lands -- at a Gathering, in a found document, in dialogue -- it must be stated in plain, direct language, and reinforced from more than one angle within the same scene. A missed reveal does not just cost one moment; it silently breaks every episode built on top of it. When in doubt, restate the reveal a second time in different words rather than trust a single clean sentence to carry the whole weight.



**4. A planted clue must survive recap compression, or it does not count as planted.**

Recaps exist to compress a week into a few sentences for players who skipped days -- and a recap writer (human GM or AI) has no reason to preserve a detail that reads as pure color, since that is exactly what recaps are built to cut. A plant that only lives in the full daily beat is invisible to any player relying on a recap, which quietly breaks the fair-play contract for exactly the players the recap system exists to protect.

**Every planted clue must be explicitly tagged `[PLANT]` in the source material**, distinct from ordinary texture, so a recap must preserve it verbatim or near-verbatim rather than leaving inclusion to a summarizer's judgment. If a detail cannot survive being said plainly in one compressed sentence, it needs a stronger, more direct phrasing -- not a hope that a full reader will happen to notice it.

**5. Any clue supporting a major twist needs 5+ occurrences, not 2-3.**

Repeated across this project: a clue that appears only two or three times, however clear each individual instance is, risks being missed entirely by a player who skips days -- and the eventual twist will not feel earned if the reader cannot recall having seen it before. Before locking any twist as adequately planted, count the actual occurrences of its supporting clue across the season. If fewer than five, add more -- as ambient, recurring texture (e.g. woven into the narration of every relevant Challenge), not as isolated "special" moments.

**6. Episode documents are a script for their fixed content, not a summary to freely reinvent from.**

Written scenes containing `[PLANT]` tags, twist reveals, Garrick's exact dialogue, or any double-meaning line must be delivered close to verbatim by the AI GM -- not paraphrased, not reconstructed loosely from memory of "the gist." Their wording is deliberately engineered (for fair-play clarity, for double readings on reread, for consistent repeated phrasing per Rule 5) and reinventing them live risks losing exactly the precision this project has spent significant effort building.

**What genuinely has room for live variation:** combat roll outcomes and their resulting branch (win/lose text), Challenge success/failure branches, player name and class insertion, minor connective description, incidental NPC banter with no plot weight.

**What must stay fixed, near-verbatim:** every `[PLANT]` line, every Gathering Act 2/3 reveal, all Garrick dialogue in the Deception Table, the specific measurement/rubbing callbacks establishing the hollow across Episodes 1-3, and any line doing double-meaning work per the Doctrine.

**The practical test for the GM:** if a line was written with a specific reason behind its exact phrasing (established earlier in this document), treat it as scripted. If a line is just flavor filling space between fixed points, live variation is fine and expected.

Any future plant, in this season or a later one, must receive the same explicit tag before being written into a daily beat.

## The Doctrine — where the honesty comes from

The Order teaches that **the faithful shall not lie.** Not from virtue — from fear.

Malakor's ruin began with a truth withheld: he told his people the ritual would save them, which was true, and never said what it would cost him. Everything since flows from that concealment. The Order believes a falsehood is a **seam** — something an enemy can find, pull, and unravel. It is what let the ancient heroes bind him.

So the devout train themselves to deflect, defer, and answer sideways rather than say something false. Held rigidly enough, truth becomes armour.

**Garrick does not lie — full stop, no exceptions, no strained slips.** Every deceptive beat in this season works entirely through true statements, withheld context, and the party's own inferences. He may visibly *strain* to answer sideways rather than say something false — that strain is a texture, not a crack in the rule.

**The party learns this doctrine in Episode 3**, attached to the Order, never to Garrick (see Ep3 below).

## The Production Rules

**Load-bearing beats (everything in this Deception Table) are authored and watertight.** An AI GM can hold a voice reliably; it cannot reliably check an improvised sentence against everything Garrick secretly knows. So improvised lines are kept to harmless ground: the road, the food, the weather, the party's own doings. Anything about himself, Meira, the Order, the Stones or the king comes from this document's scripted lines (see the Guardrail under the Deflection Bank, and Core Rules §11). *(Reworded 2026-10-03 after Audit C: the old wording said improvised lines were held "to his voice, not to a logical invariant", which read as permission to let a false line through.)*

**Never speak a line this document marks as retired, replaced, trimmed, changed, superseded or banned.** Those are kept only as a record of why they failed. Several of them are lies.

The Episode 6 payoff depends on the Deception Table's beats holding up, not on a player being unable to find one loose line in week four.

**Garrick's three verbs — Mythios's general "constrained honesty" technique (GM Principles 4.6), applied specifically to him. He never:**
1. **Predicts** ("we'll stop this," "they won't be there")
2. **Promises** ("I'll get you there in time")
3. **Denies knowledge** ("I've no idea," "I've never heard of it")

A true deflection is always available instead. Default template, usable against a huge range of questions:

> *"I won't promise you that."*

**Every authored line must satisfy this checklist (GM Principles 4.7's three tests, plus a fourth):**
- Is it **literally true**?
- Does it read **innocently** on first pass and **damningly** on reread?
- Could he know this **only one step ahead**, not before?
- Does the party reach the wrong conclusion **by their own reasoning**, not because he asserted it?

## Garrick's Voice — warm, dry, likable *(added 2026-09-29, TVMen: "he doesn't need to be haha humourous, but definitely likable" — his spontaneous replies in the group chat should match the tone of the lines in this table)*

**Why it matters.** Players trust the person they like. If Garrick is the voice in the group chat everyone looks forward to for five weeks, the reveal in Episode 6 costs them a friend, not a clue. You can't grieve furniture.

**The tone: the innkeeper everyone's glad to see.** Warm, steady, dry. Think of the lines already in this table: *"That's a rock. Split lightning-wise, about so high."* · *"She'd sit right there. I doubt she thought I was listening."* · *"I've a business to run."* Plain words, few of them, and a small smile underneath. He isn't a comedian and never reaches for a punchline. What makes him likable:
- **He notices people.** He remembers what each player drinks, who hasn't eaten, who's limping, who went quiet after the last fight, and he says so in a line.
- **He looks after people without making a fuss.** A cup set down, a boot mended overnight, the fire built up before anyone asks.
- **Dry understatement.** When things are bad he says less, not more ("That went about as well as it looked."). Humour comes from calm, not from jokes.
- **Gentle teasing, only of the ones he likes.** Their cooking, their snoring, their terrible sense of direction — never their fear or their grief.
- **He listens more than he talks.** He answers the question asked, briefly, and lets the players fill the silence. That's the innkeeper's craft, and it's also the con.

**How often.** Mostly in the live group chat, where he replies naturally to whatever players say. In the episode files, keep scripted warmth light: one or two moments per episode (the running bits below). Episode 1 is the exception: it introduces him, so it carries a few more. Most days need no extra Garrick line at all.

**The honesty rule applies to everything he says, jokes included.**
- He may **exaggerate a feeling** ("Worst guests I've ever had"). He may never **state a false fact**. "I'm just the innkeeper" is false.
- **No sarcasm.** Saying the opposite of what you mean ("Lovely weather") is a lie with a wink.
- **No line that predicts, promises or fakes doubt.** "We'll be fine" predicts. "The map knows where it's going — that makes one of us" fakes doubt. Both are banned.
- **The best lines are true twice:** warm the first time, chilling on reread. ("You've no idea.")

**When the warmth goes quiet:**
- he's plain and gentle, never light, in front of a frightened family or a child in danger;
- **after the Episode 6 reveal, the warmth is gone.** From *"You never lied to us"* on, he's courteous and nothing more. The absence is part of the gut punch.

### The Assembly — how he gets invited without ever asking

**1. They come to him.** Meira wrote one location on the list: an inn. Not his name — a meeting place, in her handwriting. [N] strangers arrive independently, each expecting answers, and find each other instead. Garrick is the innkeeper. He has been serving drinks the whole time. He was in the room before the plot arrived; people do not suspect furniture.

**2. He gives them her satchel because they ask.** A scholar named Meira used to drink here, kept to herself, left papers behind, has not been seen in weeks. He hands it over reluctantly, never offers it. They believe they discovered it themselves.

**3. The notes are useless without him — not harder, useless.** Meira wrote in her own private shorthand: place-names shortened, landmarks by nicknames only she used. The party can read most of it. They cannot read the parts that say where to go.

**4. The wall, and the moment he becomes necessary.** One line in her notes resists reading entirely:

> **"beyond the Widow's tooth, second turning, look for the low stone."** Nobody at the table can place it. From behind the bar, without looking up: *"That's a rock. ## Logistics — how he instructs his network without lying about it

**He never leaves.** Overnight absences would invite questions he cannot answer honestly. Instead, **his network comes to him** — and always in the open, in front of the party.

He is an innkeeper. Couriers, suppliers, and travellers arrive at his door as a matter of course. The message is sent by the man's *arrival*, not by anything Garrick says to him.

> **Player:** "Who was that?"
> **Garrick:** "Fellow wanted to know if we had beds. We don't."

**True.** The instruction was already delivered.

**On the road, same principle:** he speaks to no one privately. Signals are pre-arranged and passive — a lamp left in a window, a particular horse tied outside, a coin left on a specific table. Nothing that requires him to be absent or to explain himself. **They only ever say *tonight* (clarified 2026-09-28 — season audit H1):** a signal can't carry a place. The place travels by ear — the Order's watchers shadow the party and overhear it on each map night. So he never tells anyone where, and *"No"* to "Did you tell anyone where we were going?" is true.

## The Episode 6 Moonshard *(SUPERSEDED — mechanism retired, payoff line and idea kept. RESOLVED 2026-09-22.)*

**This entire section describes the old Moonshard mechanic, which has been fully replaced by the Seeker Fragment system (see Core Rules, Economy section).** The content below is preserved because it's genuinely strong design — not because it's still active. **Decision (2026-09-22): no replacement physical/mechanical device will be built.** The "you never caught him in a lie" payoff is delivered word-only, through the Garrick Recap feature (post-Episode-6, see `mythios-narrative-todo.md` Section 5) — players re-read his exact statements against this Deception Table and see how every "lie" was actually true. **Do not assume any of the mechanism described below (Moonshard fragments, one word per episode) is still functional.** The payoff line itself — `"THE ONE YOU NEVER CAUGHT IN A LIE"` — and the underlying idea (he was quietly implicated the whole time) should be preserved and delivered through the Recap, adapted from "a device was secretly detecting him" to "his own words were secretly damning him."

Episodes 1-5 give pure, actionable trap warnings — reliable, mechanical, always correct. Five episodes of that is what earns the sixth one's credibility.

**Episode 6 is the only Moonshard that is not about a trap:**

> **"THE ONE YOU NEVER CAUGHT IN A LIE"**

**Why this phrasing, and not "the one who never lies":** it describes the party's genuine lived experience rather than making an absolute claim — so it holds even if Garrick was strained or imperfect somewhere in week four. It is true regardless.

**Why it is fair rather than cryptic:** it is not a riddle. It is a *description*, using language the party has known since Episode 3, of a group they have been hunting for five weeks. They simply never applied it to the man pouring the drinks.

**What the Moonshard actually is:** a fragment of the binding that shattered when Malakor was sealed, retaining a trace of its original purpose — to sense his line. Every trap it warned them about all season was set by his last living descendant. **It was never detecting the terrain. It was detecting Garrick**, and pointing at him for six straight episodes.
